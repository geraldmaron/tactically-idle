# Technique library

Screen and game techniques for a call the player meets in about ninety seconds. Each entry says
what it does, why it works with a source tag from [sources.md](sources.md), a pattern line
written for this game, the stage and field it lands in, and how it goes wrong.

Makers and works are named only as the source of a technique, never as a voice to imitate.
Nothing here quotes a source beyond a short phrase. Every pattern line fits the typed-lane word
lists and budgets (checked against a copy of the lint patterns on 2026-10-06), and every one is
illustrative. Never paste a pattern line into data. Write the call's own.

## Hooks

### T1. Ordinary moment, one wrong note

- Does. Starts inside a routine and breaks it with one detail that does not fit.
- Works because. The routine reads at a glance, and the anomaly opens a question the mind wants
  closed [TV14][TV4][PS1].
- Pattern. "The porch light has been on for three days, and the mail is still in the box."
- Lands in. Card hook, sentence one (`opening` or `summary`).
- Goes wrong. Two anomalies at once, or the anomaly explained in the same line.

### T2. Steal the headline, invent the body

- Does. Borrows a recognizable call type and invents every particular.
- Works because. A familiar premise buys belief, and invented specifics keep the turn available
  and exploit no real victim [TV1].
- Pattern. "A tenant served notice this morning will not open his door to the bailiff."
- Lands in. Sheet row B, then `title` and `opening`.
- Goes wrong. Re-creating an incident a reader could identify (E8).

### T3. Let the listener draw the picture

- Does. Gives the briefing as fragments someone heard or saw, and lets the player fill in the
  image.
- Works because. An imagined picture is personal and vivid, and a wrong first impression
  becomes the turn [TV8].
- Pattern. "The boy on the phone says his mom is sleeping and the TV is very loud."
- Lands in. `known` lines, `approachResults`, map markers.
- Goes wrong. The caller knows more than a caller could.

### T4. Turn a familiar scene around

- Does. Puts the player on the side of a common event that cop stories skip.
- Works because. The familiar frame reads instantly, and the new angle makes it worth reading
  [IF10].
- Pattern. "The courier just wants a signature so he can finish his route."
- Lands in. The person card's want.
- Goes wrong. The inversion turns into a lecture.

## Pressure

### T5. A clock needs a reason nobody can stop

- Does. Ties the deadline to something outside anyone's control.
- Works because. A real deadline turns each decision into a trade [TV7].
- Pattern. "Shift change is at 6. Forty workers will walk through that lobby."
- Lands in. The hand-authored pressure block and `pressureLabel`. Typed calls need R3.
- Goes wrong. A clock with no cause, or any clock promised on a typed call.

### T6. Stretch the wait, release in one line

- Does. Holds the player in a pause, then ends it in a single plain sentence.
- Works because. Waiting builds more tension than action, and contrast makes the release land
  [TV9][TV10].
- Pattern. "The door is open an inch. The TV inside goes quiet."
- Lands in. The hold stage prompt, then one result line.
- Goes wrong. Describing force, or a release that runs past one sentence (E2).

### T7. Cut the hallway

- Does. Opens every stage at the new position, already mid-problem.
- Works because. Travel is not story [TV1].
- Pattern. "At the back door. The lock is new, and the frame is not."
- Lands in. The first words of each stage prompt.
- Goes wrong. "The team moves to the second floor."

### T8. The bomb under the table

- Does. Tells the player what is at risk early, and spends surprise once.
- Works because. Shared foreknowledge sustains suspense, while a surprise buys a moment [PS10].
- Pattern. "The landlord has the only key, and he is in another state."
- Lands in. Card and briefing for the risk. `confirmed`, `disproved` or a `truth` branch for
  the one surprise.
- Goes wrong. A twist on the card, or two twists.

### T9. Answer what the card asks

- Does. Makes the debrief answer every question the card raised.
- Works because. Questions that get answered keep the player's trust [TV7].
- Pattern. "The light was on a timer. She has been at her daughter’s since Sunday."
- Lands in. `confirmed`, `disproved` and the ending summary.
- Goes wrong. Holding the card's question back as the open loop. The loop is a new thread.

## The person at the center

### T10. Fear, then who, then why

- Does. Starts from a behavior that frightens, then the person, then the motive.
- Works because. Motive turns a threat into a person and keeps a question alive past the action
  [TV4][TV5].
- Pattern. "Someone leaves the lights on in empty units, one floor higher each night."
- Lands in. The card names the behavior, the briefing gives two or three behavioral reads, the
  debrief answers why.
- Goes wrong. A monster in place of a person, or the motive spent on the card.

### T11. Everyone believes they are justified

- Does. Gives each person a private logic the text obeys and never states.
- Works because. A coherent person is frightening and reachable at once, and people lie to
  themselves [TV3].
- Pattern (never printed). "If it’s a break-in, someone will ask why the back lock has stuck
  since spring."
- Lands in. Person card, private logic. It surfaces only as behavior in results.
- Goes wrong. Printing the logic, or a villain who knows he is one.

### T12. One true procedural detail per stage, written as a decision

- Does. Shows how the work goes in one clause that changes a choice.
- Works because. Insider detail reads as truth, and working it out is a pleasure
  [TV15][TV11][TV20].
- Pattern. "The hinges are on the outside, so the door opens toward you."
- Lands in. A stage prompt or a choice summary.
- Goes wrong. A glossary line, or a method (E1).

## Character under pressure

### T13. The finely balanced test

- Does. Sets two goods or two costs so close that the choice reveals what the player values.
- Works because. Only a finely balanced choice tests anyone [TV3].
- Pattern. "He has stopped answering, but he still holds the phone. The team is at the stairs."
- Lands in. Every row of the stage table.
- Goes wrong. One option better on every axis.

### T14. Interesting over likable

- Does. Gives a person one ordinary detail and one contradiction, not a list of virtues.
- Works because. Players bond with specifics and contradiction, and a camera held long enough
  makes people root for anyone [TV6][TV13].
- Pattern. "She keeps the shop open past ten for one regular and has never asked his name."
- Lands in. Person cards. Officers belong to swat-officer-stories.
- Goes wrong. A resume, or a point of view that launders misconduct (E10).

### T15. The ensemble disagrees

- Does. Stages the dilemma through two professionals who give command different clocks.
- Works because. Disagreement inside the team shows the stakes without a narrator, and good
  policing slows things while drama wants speed [TV2][TV12][AU1].
- Pattern. "One officer wants to wait for the negotiator. Another says the smoke changes
  everything."
- Lands in. The briefing recommendation slot (design only until R4).
- Goes wrong. One side written as wrong, or the disagreement placed in a choice label.

## Consequence

### T16. Violence leaves a bill

- Does. Carries injury and strain past the call.
- Works because. Consequences the player feels make the next choice heavy [TV2][TV17].
- Pattern. "Two days of light duty for the medic. The debrief names the call."
- Lands in. `officerHarm`, the debrief, the roster (with swat-officer-stories).
- Goes wrong. Injury as decoration, or gore (E7).

### T17. The mystery first, then the moral cost

- Does. Solves the problem, then shows what it cost a person or the institution.
- Works because. A cost shown after the solve keeps the story going [TV1][TV11][TV3].
- Pattern. "Command asks why the street was shut for six hours."
- Lands in. Stage 3 or the debrief.
- Goes wrong. A sermon, or reassurance that all is well (P7).

### T18. Save the ones nobody likes

- Does. Values the life of the person who caused the call.
- Works because. Valuing life without conditions is a stronger ethic than rewarding nice
  victims [TV14].
- Pattern. "He swore at every officer on the stairs. The crew takes him out awake and talking."
- Lands in. Endings. The best grade includes the subject alive (E9).
- Goes wrong. Grading only the sympathetic people.

### T19. Research from people who live it

- Does. Draws on doctrine where time is generally on the responders' side.
- Works because. One lived detail outweighs ten generic ones, and the de-escalation training
  holds that distance and cover buy time [TV18][TV1][TV19][TV20].
- Pattern. "Distance and cover buy time. The team holds at the corner and keeps him talking."
- Lands in. Choices where waiting is workable, and sometimes best.
- Goes wrong. Invented procedure presented as real.

## Interactive structure

### T20. Branch and bottleneck

- Does. Rejoins branches at shared stages and lets tracked state carry the difference.
- Works because. It keeps authoring finite while choices still matter, as long as the rejoin
  remembers [IF1][IF3].
- Pattern. "The crew you called at the start is two minutes out."
- Lands in. Stage 3 prompts and `contextPrompts`. Typed calls need R8.
- Goes wrong. The rejoin forgets the choice (checklist line 7).

### T21. Escalating commitment

- Does. Carries one dilemma across three stages, each raising the stake of the last.
- Works because. Choices gain weight when the player commits again and again [IF2].
- Pattern. "You went in on the manager’s word. Now the tenant is asking who let you in."
- Lands in. The stage table, read top to bottom.
- Goes wrong. A fresh, unrelated dilemma at every stage.

### T22. Peak and end

- Does. Writes the ending last and best, on a person's state.
- Works because. People judge an experience by its worst moment and its last one [PS4].
- Pattern. "Forty minutes on the porch. He came out carrying the dog."
- Lands in. Ending summaries.
- Goes wrong. A stat tally, or a lesson.

### T23. Foreseeable, not predictable

- Does. Puts the cue for every bad result on screen before the choice.
- Works because. Players accept a loss they chose with open eyes, and resent one that
  contradicts the intent they chose with [IF5].
- Pattern. Cue "Staff say the boy came in with his dad." Result "He is the father. The boy
  cries harder."
- Lands in. The consequence map, cue column.
- Goes wrong. An adverse result with no cue.

### T24. Failure opens story

- Does. Makes a setback change the situation rather than end it.
- Works because. Players enjoy bad things happening when it moves the story, and a gauntlet
  that just ends feels punishing [IF9][IF16][IF1].
- Pattern. "The ladder is too short for the second floor. She watches the team go back for
  another."
- Lands in. Adverse effects.
- Goes wrong. Failure that only lowers a number.

### T25. Odds with a picture

- Does. Pairs every forecast with one concrete image of the bad case.
- Works because. Players judge odds by feel, not arithmetic [IF18].
- Pattern. "Fast. If he’s behind the door, he hears the ram first."
- Lands in. Summaries and outcome previews.
- Goes wrong. "This may or may not work."

### T26. Feedback informs

- Does. Tells the player what their choice did and which skill or gear mattered.
- Works because. Information supports competence, and scolding undercuts autonomy [PS5].
- Pattern. "Talking first kept him at the window. The medic’s training opened the care step."
- Lands in. The debrief.
- Goes wrong. Grading the player's character.
