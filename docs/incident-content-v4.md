# Incident content version 4

New generated calls use v4. Issued v1–v3 IDs and generated definitions retain their original builders. The three legacy decision-exercise IDs remain readable; six v4 exercises are listed in practice. No issued card is silently regenerated with the new content.

## Three base decision families, nine public premises

| Family | Public premise | What changes in play |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Welfare/report | Conflicting intruder account | Comparing independent timelines can settle the claims and close a false call in three decisions |
| Welfare/report | Welfare after a reported threat | Person starts located; agreeing how to communicate helps a difficult conversation and a voluntary next step |
| Welfare/report | Alarm account in doubt | A verified callback can settle the reported person/threat, with a clean mistaken-report ending |
| Medical assistance | Care during an ongoing response | Optional trained first aid reduces time pressure; it never substitutes for paramedic assessment |
| Medical assistance | Interrupted medical conversation | Communication preparation improves agreement, with care or voluntary follow-up determined by checked needs |
| Medical assistance | Care after the protective phase | Location and ended threat start known; the local ambulance is unavailable and a district crew has a longer arrival time |
| Protective | Barricade dialogue | The team's own negotiation qualification improves its plan; a failed plan opens reassessment and one revised attempt |
| Protective | Agreed move needs a usable route | Cooperation can establish agreement immediately, but the proposed exit must be usable; reassessment can instead prepare another agreed plan |
| Protective | Changed threat account | An independent account can rule out the threat and support a proportionate early ending |

Every briefing says why SWAT was requested or why the team is already at the scene. Routine alarms, medical complaints and distress are not deployment reasons by themselves. Public premise, medical resource capacity and hidden facts use separate seed purposes. Presence, current medical concern, immediate danger and exit suitability are distinct facts.

## Three additional high-risk patterns

New v4 calls also include active armed incidents, hostage crises and protected rescues. These use separate builders with individual civilian outcomes, qualified equipment choices, reserved vehicle support where physically usable, and explicit officer casualty decisions. The six practice entries expose all six decision families immediately. The new draw pool gives these three patterns additional weight; the old v1–v3 draw streams remain unchanged.

- An adverse armed response can leave danger active and wound an officer. One new regrouped plan can continue with the remaining qualified team; it does not reset the failed roll. A checked pause and later dialogue are separate paths. Less-lethal equipment only becomes an option in an appropriate, checked post-interruption context.
- A hostage release accounts for two people separately. Protecting one does not finish the second person's objective.
- A rescue can use an assigned, serviceable armored vehicle with a qualified driver and accessible exterior staging, or a separately checked slower assistance route. Rescue checks use awareness, coordination and composure, not shooting.
- People who are safe and have no current medical need can agree on their next step and finish without an ambulance. A recorded injury overrides an earlier report of no medical complaint.

## Officer injuries and civilian outcomes

An authored injury affects a named officer who actually took part. Wounded officers stop contributing skills, qualifications, mentoring, vehicle operation or squad support. Command-level requests and recorded withdrawal remain available if no deployed officer can continue field work.

Qualified first aid uses one trauma kit for one untreated officer and shortens the game recovery timer once. The officer remains out of action; an available named ambulance must explicitly accept the medical transfer. Direct professional transfer stays possible without a first aider or kit. Other duties may continue with the reduced team, but unhandled casualties block full completion.

Recovery lasts two real hours for a wound and eight for a serious wound, reduced by 20% once after successful field care. These are game-balance values. Practice changes its simulated run only. Real operations save the injury and recovery deadline, and debriefs retain their own injury snapshots. Civilian status separately records unaccounted people, people needing help, safety, actual injury and accepted care; the general safety score never invents a casualty or proves everybody was accounted for.

## Support and completion

The named City or District paramedic crew has a public arrival time and availability. A request starts its operation-time clock without objective credit. Other useful preparation can happen while the crew travels. One waiting choice advances exactly the remaining time, and cannot be replayed after arrival. Crew arrival, physical access and agreement are separate from acceptance of care.

Care completes only when the available named crew reaches the person and accepts responsibility, with the team’s immediate safety and access duties met. This has the same full objective credit as completing a protective plan or a verified voluntary next step. Unavailable crews and blocked access produce honest pending or unresolved outcomes. The engine limits incomplete rewards even if safety remains intact.

A protective setback does not end the incident. Reassessment preserves existing preparation and opens one new plan. It does not reset a used action. Every action is one-shot, and every stage retains a way to progress or record unfinished work. Care arrangements replace the ordinary response choices rather than appending a large menu.

## Design grounding

These are fictional game situations. General role checks came from [Seattle Police's SWAT overview](https://www.seattle.gov/police/about-us/about-policing/swat), [Seattle Fire's Medic One description](https://www.seattle.gov/fire/about-us/about-the-department/operations/medic-one), [FEMA's law-enforcement resource description](https://rtlt.preptoolkit.fema.gov/Public/Resource/View/6-508-1245?a=False&p=3&s=Name), and [DOJ's law-enforcement communication guidance](https://www.ada.gov/resources/law-enforcement-guide/). The point is believable game choices, not an operational training simulation.
